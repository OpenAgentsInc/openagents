## What the adapter did

Written after the run. Both doors answer the same 196-item suite through the
same `crates/jev` client; only the evaluation split is scored, and the
adapter never saw it.

| | Base | Adapted | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Accuracy | 0.77 | **0.90** | +13 points |
| Brier | 0.154 | **0.103** | better |
| ECE (raw) | 0.106 | 0.097 | about the same |
| Log loss (raw) | 1.952 | 2.323 | **worse** |
| Confident errors (raw) | 6 | 8 | **worse** |

Per family, raw accuracy:

| Family | Base | Adapted |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `routing` | 0.82 | **0.92** |
| `severity` | 0.67 | **0.78** |
| `urgency` | 0.73 | **0.93** |

**The adapter works.** Thirteen points of accuracy on items it was not
trained on, and every family improves. Brier falls by a third, which means it
is both more often right and more willing to commit. Two hours of setup and
four minutes of training on 98 records bought that.

It also cleared the calibration bar. The base model gets one family admitted
(`routing`); the adapted model gets two (`severity` and `urgency`), and its
`routing` map is refused only because the raw signal improved so much —
0.140 down to 0.075 — that there is no longer a tenth of headroom left for a
binned table to find.

**And it is more overconfident, which the accuracy number hides.** Log loss
rose from 1.952 to 2.323 and confident errors went from six to eight. That is
the ordinary signature of supervised fine-tuning on a small corpus: the model
learned the task and also learned to be sure. The two measures that punish
confident wrongness both got worse while the two that reward being right both
got better.

The comparison after calibration is where it stings. Base with its admitted
map reaches log loss 0.713 with **one** confident error. Adapted with its two
admitted maps sits at 1.115 with **nine**. So on the question "how often does
this thing assert something false at high confidence," the *base* model plus
a fitted map is currently the better product, even though the adapted model
is right far more often.

### What to do about it

- **Ship the adapter for the decision, not yet for the probability.** At 0.90
  against 0.77 it is clearly the better answer. Its numbers are not yet the
  better confidence.
- **Train the band next.** This run trained `choice` only. The `--band`
  objective labels the certainty field from measured base accuracy per item,
  which is exactly the signal missing here — a model that says `even odds`
  before it is wrong. The base rates it needs are the per-item results this
  run just produced.
- **Try fewer epochs.** Four epochs on 98 records is where overconfidence
  comes from. `adapter-epoch2.pt` is kept; scoring it is one command and
  would separate "the adapter is overconfident" from "this recipe is."
- **Do not widen the admission gate to make the adapted numbers look better.**
  It was widened once already, on its own merits. The right response to nine
  confident errors is a better model, not a looser rule.

### Caveats worth keeping

98 training records and 98 evaluation items. The labels are the author's. The
suite is English. A thirteen-point gain on one small in-domain suite is
evidence that the mechanism works end to end, not evidence about how this
adapter behaves on a workload it has never seen.

## Order sensitivity

`lev-permute`, now `gym permute`, runs every Choice item forward and with its option list
reversed, greedy so each pass is one deterministic call. Evaluation split
only — running over every item mixes in records the adapter trained on and
inflates the accuracy columns.

| Door | Items | Flips | Flip rate | Accuracy forward | Accuracy reversed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `lev-base` | 50 | 6 | 0.120 | 0.84 | 0.84 |
| `lev-adapted` | 50 | 6 | 0.120 | 0.92 | 0.88 |

**Fine-tuning did not fix order sensitivity.** Both doors flip 12% of their
greedy answers when the option list is reversed — identical rates, and worse
than kev-0.5b's 7.4% across four permutations or hosted Jev's zero observed
flips.

**And the adapter picked up a little order dependence of its own.** The base
scores the same in both directions, 0.84 and 0.84. The adapted model scores
0.92 forward and 0.88 reversed, so about a third of its gain is specific to
the order the options were presented in during training. It is still ahead in
both directions, which is what matters, but the asymmetry is real and it
comes from the training data: every record presented its options in one
fixed order.

The fix is the one kev already uses — shuffle option order as an augmentation
when converting the suite, so the same item appears under several
permutations. That is a change to `convert.py`, not to the recipe, and it
should land before the next training run.

For comparison, across the full suite including training items the rates are
the same: base 0.110 over 100 items, adapted 0.120. The flip rate does not
distinguish memorised items from unseen ones, which is itself a small piece
of evidence that the flips are a decoding property rather than a knowledge
gap.
