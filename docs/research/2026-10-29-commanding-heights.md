# The Commanding Heights: what we should learn from it

- Source: Will Manidis and Joe Roberts, "The Commanding Heights",
  <https://commanding-heights.com/>. Markdown edition:
  <https://commanding-heights.com/commanding-heights.md>.
- Published 2026-10-29, version 1.0. About 40,000 words, 153 notes, 27
  figures. The authors mark it `ai-input=yes, ai-train=yes`.
- Read in full for this report on 2026-10-29.

## The argument in one paragraph

New technologies rarely make their biggest fortunes where they are invented.
Gutenberg went broke. The railroad fortunes went to the people who owned the
routes and the finance, not the people who built locomotives. Standard Oil was
broken up for owning the pipelines, not for finding oil. The authors expect
AI to follow the same pattern. Frontier models cost billions to build, and
within months they become commodities priced at the cost of the compute that
runs them. The labs, chips and data centres (which the essay calls
**Genesis**) will keep spending on themselves even as returns fall. The
lasting wealth goes to **Commanding Heights**: "scarce, controllable positions
of economic power whose holders durably capture the gains of increasingly
capable and diffused intelligence." Their summary line: *"In the world to
come, one either owns the rails or pays those who do."*

## How the essay decides what counts as a Height

The essay doesn't put this as a checklist, but its tests are clear enough to
use as one:

1. **Is it scarce?** Something that intelligence has to pass through to reach
   its use.
2. **Does it last?** Margins jump to the next bottleneck. A Height is the one
   they cannot route around. The question is always what the next-best route
   costs.
3. **Can the holder control it?** Through ownership, contract length and
   terms.
4. **Does it grow with the economy it serves?** The toll rises as activity
   multiplies, and "Their valuations need not ever rise for their cash flows
   to multiply."
5. **Does revenue rise with nominal activity without matching capex?** This is
   the protection against inflation. A channel's revenue inflates, but the
   cost of renewing its machinery does not.
6. **Can it survive political claims on its rent?** That means regulators,
   ratepayers, local boards (Loudoun, Pittsylvania) and, last of all, the
   sovereign. "Antitrust is a map of where wealth concentrates."

The essay also names several things that are **not** Heights:

- model margins;
- passing hardware scarcity;
- services roll-ups and forward-deployed engineering ("DeployCos"), which are
  limited by how many people they can hire;
- businesses valued on revenue multiples that the next model update could
  wipe out, unless they hold proprietary data;
- the last era's compounders.

## What this means for OpenAgents

We are reading this from a repo that was reset on 2026-10-27 to a small,
checked core:

- the Coax language;
- the Metareal engine;
- one OpenAgents Protocol;
- Nostr as the open transport;
- a native iOS app;
- next, a Bitcoin-only Lightning wallet with its payment rules in checked
  Coax.

Here is what the essay says about each of those choices.

### 1. Being outside Genesis is a strength, as long as we stay model-agnostic

We don't train frontier models, and the essay says that is the layer where
margins fall to the cost of compute. Its picture of the toll-holder is
someone who is "model-agnostic and whatever enables the daily occurrences of
activity within his domain will be sufficient." Lessons:

- Nothing we build should depend on a particular model's price or lead.
  Route by price and capability, and expect a commodity floor near the cost
  of compute, set by open weights.
- Treat any margin we earn from reselling inference as temporary.

### 2. The essay's rail is a dollar stablecoin, and ours is Bitcoin

The essay treats the money rail as the strongest Height: USDT in a wallet on
a messaging app, with the issuer earning interest on the reserves and the
channel owner taking a cut. Bitcoin never appears in the essay. That gap
matters to us:

- **Where we agree.** People in weak-currency economies want dollar-stable
  savings, and agents will be paid and will pay in that unit. Taproot Assets
  stablecoins on Lightning give us a dollar unit without leaving our Bitcoin
  rule. That makes them the piece most likely to matter for users like the
  essay's Malena, and they should not be an afterthought to the sats wallet.
- **Where we differ.** The essay describes dollar-holders as targets. The
  sovereign "can tax your wealth" through debasement, and the many who save
  will "bear the primary losses." A self-custodial Bitcoin wallet is the
  obvious way out of that. Whether a user holds sats or a dollar token
  should be the user's choice, and the wallet should make that choice and
  its cost clear.
- **Open problem.** The essay assumes a "toll that rises with inflation"
  goes to the owner of the channel. Lightning and Nostr are open protocols
  designed so that no one owns the channel. We need to say plainly where
  OpenAgents captures value without becoming a toll gate. Candidates:
  - the installed base (the app people open every day);
  - trust in checked money code;
  - paid verification;
  - markets for agent work.

### 3. Distribution is the Height, and it arrives in an app people already use

In the essay, intelligence reaches people through "a software update", "a
ping on her phone", and "notifications from the global messaging app." The
final Height it names is "a population-scale installed base." For us:

- The iOS app and Verse only matter as a Height if people open them every
  day for things they already do, with money moving in the same place.
- A clever demo is not that. The questions to measure are whether people
  come back every day and whether money moves in the app.

### 4. When intelligence is abundant, checked and liable work gains value

The essay predicts that licensing and surety gain value because "a liable
human must remain responsible for the output." It also predicts an ideal
regulator that looks like "a machine intelligent Lloyd's of London",
underwriters who price risk. Coax puts the checks inside the language:

- `#test` and `#forall` run while compiling;
- `#authority` makes every procedure in a file need a check;
- `coax mutate` confirms the checks catch real changes.

That is a product position, not only an engineering habit. Verified money
code, together with receipts someone can audit, is something that becomes
scarcer as generated code becomes abundant. We should be able to show it to
a user or an underwriter, not only keep it inside the repo.

### 5. Ravi's story is a warning about approval UX

Ravi "signs off on without reading", and his approvals were "the training
environment for the agent that replaced him." Any approval flow we ship for
agent work (payments, deploys, Coder output) faces the same thing:

- An approval nobody reads is not oversight. Approval screens should be
  rare, specific, and backed by checks.
- If approval traces become training data, say so and decide who owns them.
  Our public `/trace/{uuid}` pages are exactly the kind of data the essay
  says labs will pay for.

### 6. The fourth settlement is how we already work

The essay's firm is "a few operators, paid and governed like capital,
directing work done mostly by models." That is the OpenAgents operating
model:

- Foreman dispatching agents;
- Coder working through the issue backlog;
- an owner reviewing results.

The essay's point about pay applies to us too. A small number of operators
and agents produce most of the value, so attribution and payouts should
follow measured contribution. Anything that pays agents or contributors
(Pylon-style payouts, bounties) should be built on that.

### 7. Data and whole-firm training environments

"If your firm's primary value comes from its data, you'll want to sell." The
essay says labs will buy entire businesses as RL environments, and cites
Google and Mercor bidding for Spirit Airlines' operational data. Our agent
traces, issue→PR history and verified Coax test corpus are environment-shaped
data. We should decide deliberately what we publish openly, what we keep, and
what we would license. The essay's own metadata licenses it for training,
which is a deliberate distribution choice.

### 8. Expect our customers' world to be K-shaped

The essay predicts:

- concentrated wealth at the top;
- jobless young entrants;
- an end to the offshore-labour route out of poorer economies;
- more scams run from the emptied office parks.

For a payments and agent product that means:

- design for adversarial users early (pig-butchering over chat, agent-driven
  fraud);
- treat cheap, global, phone-first access as the main market, not an edge
  case;
- keep the money rules strict, which is the reason they live in checked Coax.

## Where to be sceptical

- **It is an investment pitch.** Part VIII ends by telling allocators to
  "reconstruct" their portfolios and "re-mandate" managers, and it redacts
  the company it thinks is the next Google Ads. Read the conclusions as the
  authors' position.
- **Rhetoric does much of the work.** There is scripture, Lenin, a fictional
  narrator and the repeated line "the line must go up." Several central
  predictions are offered as inevitable rather than argued:
  - the Fed will cap yields again;
  - labs will split into a commercial company and a sovereign lab;
  - frontier training will be licensed.
- **It leaves Bitcoin and open protocols out.** It assumes every rail has an
  owner who charges a toll. It never considers rails with no owner, such as
  open protocols or self-custody, either as competition or as a way to hedge
  against the debasement it predicts.
- **The USDT example is odd.** It holds up USDT as the American "Neo-Dollar"
  without noting that Tether is not a US-regulated issuer.
- **Fiction carries the moral weight.** The Bangalore riots and Malena losing
  her flat are illustrations, not evidence.
- **The China argument is strong but rests on one variable.** It explains
  China's open-weight strategy almost entirely by household asset mix. The
  data points it uses are worth keeping: Kimi K3, the OpenRouter share, and
  DeepSeek's "reasonable profit" call.

## Signals the essay says to watch

- Quarterly disclosures of offshore-vendor headcount cuts next to rising
  parent-company margins (the TCS pattern).
- Under-25 unemployment in AI-exposed occupations, in the US and in
  labour-exporting countries.
- The price spread between the US commercial frontier and the Chinese
  open-weight floor, and the Chinese share of routed tokens.
- Hyperscaler free cash flow, especially in 2027.
- The 10-year yield staying above ~4.5%, and any move toward capping yields.
- Federal equity stakes, golden shares, or pre-release review of frontier
  models.
- Local denials of data-centre and power permits.
- Stablecoin settlement volume in weak-currency economies.
- Agents moving household deposits toward the highest yield, which the essay
  calls the end of the bank "deposit franchise".

## Questions for us

1. Which of our layers could pass the six tests above: the wallet, the
   protocol, the app, verified Coax, or agent-work markets? Which of them are
   only Genesis-adjacent?
2. If open protocols mean we can't take a toll on the rail, what is the
   durable thing we own? Our working guess is daily use plus trusted money
   code.
3. Should the Lightning wallet ship with a Taproot Assets dollar balance
   from the start, as the answer for Malena-type users?
4. What is our policy on agent-trace and approval data: publish, keep, or
   license?
5. How do approval screens stay meaningful when the agent is right 99.9% of
   the time?
