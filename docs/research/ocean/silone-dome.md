# Ben Silone's floating concrete dome: post and video analysis

Source: <https://x.com/bsilone/status/2123066174837526859>
Posted November 18, 2026, 8:49 a.m., in the thread under Palmer Luckey's SLAB
announcement ([palmer-luckey-post.md](palmer-luckey-post.md)).

Silone is not known to be part of SLAB. The SLAB report ([slab.md](slab.md),
section 3) already flags that the airform-and-concrete design in this thread
is his, not Luckey's. Read everything below as one outside designer's concept.

## The post, verbatim

> I’ve run through countless designs and concepts for floating structures over the years.
>
> This is what I’ve put the most engineering into for the following reasons:
>
> 1. It’s a simple dome, something that has been built on land by Monolithic with airforms and shotcrete for 50+ years.
>
> 2. It’s easy to inflate such an airform on the water with a rigid ring around the base. Just like on land there is an airlock to be able to spray the inside with high strength concrete, then when cured the airform is removed and reused.
>
> 3. It maximizes above water exterior area for the material required. Towers need to be spar style with a majority of the area below water (good for compute though), rings maximize exterior area and can scale more but with a higher material cost to achieve the same level of stability. This could be a bit more cylindrical at the base to take waves head on to further reduce pitch, though that requires more structure for the airform.
>
> 4. It can be built as a single monolithic structure of about 400ft diameter that is very stable in the worst equatorial wave conditions (no hurricanes,  max recorded wave height of 17ft).
>
> 5. They can be easily joined and removed, allowing for flexible cities layouts as they scale.
>
> 6. Circular base is ideal for open ocean where waves can come from any direction and turning large heavy structures is difficult and slow.
>
> 7. The airform can easily be modified for less symmetrical custom designs.

### What the frames show about the design

- **Shape.** A low, round, white structure. A plain band of hull sits just
  above the waterline. Above it are four or five stepped, stacked terraces,
  so the dome reads as a stepped ziggurat rather than a smooth shell. A wide
  central stair runs up the front. A glass cylinder on top (a lift or lantern)
  is surrounded by palms. Every terrace is lined with planters.
- **The dome inside.** The interiors fit inside a curved shell. The gym wall
  and its lights follow a long curve. Ceilings in the residence slope down
  toward the outer glass, which is what rooms under a dome would look like.
  The large hall is a central circular atrium with rings of floors around it.
- **Uses shown.** Shops or a public hall, offices, a gym and a luxury
  residence. All of them are residential or commercial.
- **Scale.** The people in the atrium put it at about three storeys high and
  several tens of metres across. Nothing in the video gives the overall
  diameter, so the post's ~400 ft (~120 m) figure cannot be checked from it.
  The exterior looks plausible at that size.

## Relevance to SLAB

- **It's a different product.** The render is a resort-style home-and-office
  dome. Luckey says SLAB is for heavy industry: metal processing, refining,
  chemicals and desalination. A domed shell with a large central volume can
  hold some light industry, data halls and desalination. Heavy process plant
  and tank farms usually want flat, open deck.
- **The construction idea is the part worth studying.** Monolithic Dome
  Institute's airform and shotcrete domes are a real, decades-old building
  method on land. The open questions offshore are:
  - forming and spraying concrete on water;
  - whether a thin shell stays watertight and survives wave bending;
  - the rigid base ring;
  - joining domes;
  - inspecting and repairing below the waterline.

  These are the same kinds of problems the SLAB report lists for any large
  floating hull: wave flexing, damage stability and maintenance.
- **It points to tropical water.** Silone designs for the calm equatorial
  belt, with no hurricanes and a 17 ft record wave. That is the same band
  where OTEC can work (it needs about a 20 °C surface-to-deep difference).
  The design and that power source therefore point to the same locations.
  Both conflict with the "three miles off California" idea from Luckey's
  replies.
- **Spar towers for compute.** Silone's aside that spar towers, mostly
  underwater, are "good for compute" fits the cold-water cooling idea behind
  ocean data centers: there is more hull in contact with cool water.
