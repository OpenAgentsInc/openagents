# SLAB: Palmer Luckey’s offshore industrial platform

**SLAB is a proposed offshore base for American industry, with ambitions extending across manufacturing, processing, medicine, water, and potentially computing. My assessment is that its general engineering premise is credible, while its specific promises—lower costs than land, a 1,000-year design life, and relief from state and local regulation—remain publicly unverified.**

I researched the announcement and follow-up replies, an earlier recorded interview, relevant maritime law, engineering precedents, and offshore computing projects. The downloadable report contains **15 pages and 30 references**.

[SLAB_Research_Report_2026-11-18.pdf](SLAB_Research_Report_2026-11-18.pdf)

*Research current as of November 18, 2026.*

## 1. What SLAB actually is—and what has been disclosed

In the announcement you supplied, Luckey describes a seastead intended to provide industrial space at lower cost than land while avoiding state and local restrictions that he believes impede American industry. His named applications include metal processing, medical research and treatment, desalination, oil refining, and chemical engineering.

The post presents the project as longstanding work funded initially with resources from the Oculus sale. He says he is discussing it now because journalists learned of additional financing and were preparing to characterize it as his new floating AI data center project. That objection leaves room for data centers within the broader industrial concept. [X post](https://x.com/PalmerLuckey/status/2122861334371180795)

The important distinction is between **what Luckey has stated** and **what independent evidence establishes**:

| Topic | What has been disclosed | What remains unknown |
|---|---|---|
| Origins | Luckey connects development to the 2014 Oculus sale. | A documented development chronology and contemporaneous project records. |
| Financing in 2017 | He says he and friends funded the project at a **$1 billion valuation**. | Amount invested, transaction terms, ownership, and independent valuation evidence. |
| Recent financing | He says the group recently contributed additional money. | Amount, investors, current valuation, and intended spending. |
| Structural lifespan | A **1,000-year design life** is the stated target. | The component covered, materials, maintenance assumptions, tests, and independent review. |
| Industrial purpose | Several industrial sectors are contemplated. | The first customer and initial operating configuration. |
| Regulatory objective | Relief from state and local constraints. | A selected jurisdiction, specific exemptions, and agency acceptance. |
| Progress | He claims significant progress while acknowledging substantial work remains. | Publicly documented prototypes, construction, permits, procurement, or commissioning dates. |

### The $1 billion figure needs careful interpretation

**A $1 billion valuation does not mean $1 billion was raised, invested, or spent.** The announcement supplies no capital amount and does not specify whether the valuation was before or after investment.

Similarly, Facebook’s approximately $2 billion announced acquisition price for Oculus was the purchase price for the company, rather than a statement of Luckey’s personal proceeds. [about.fb.com](https://about.fb.com/news/2014/03/facebook-to-acquire-oculus/)

In the public materials I located, I could not establish:

- SLAB’s incorporated entity, investor list, or engineering leadership.
- A dedicated official website or verified explanation of the name.
- Dimensions, displacement, materials, or construction method.
- A selected location, mooring arrangement, or classification society.
- A power supply, utility agreement, or fiber route.
- Independently documented tests, construction contracts, customers, or operating results.
- A formal relationship with Anduril.

These are limits of the available public record, not proof that the information or work does not exist privately.

### The image is concept art

Your screenshot explicitly labels the image as AI-generated. It establishes the visual ambition, but it cannot establish the hull’s geometry, material, scale, freeboard, or engineering feasibility. In particular, it should not be interpreted as evidence of a completed platform.

## 2. An earlier interview supports the broader industrial vision

I found a useful additional primary source: Luckey’s interview with Walter Isaacson on *The Builders*. The publisher says it was **recorded February 10, 2026**, although published November 17.

Near the end, Luckey discusses combining power generation, manufacturing, processing, and recycling to reduce the cost of goods. He then discusses ocean settlement and expects materials advances to enable very durable marine megastructures. The interview does not name SLAB or reveal its technical design. [iHeart](https://www.iheart.com/podcast/1119-the-builders-347224769/episode/palmer-luckey-steve-jobs-ruined-everything-350178888/)

This supports his account of a broader interest that predates the current disclosure. It does not independently verify the 2014 start, 2017 financing, construction progress, or claimed materials breakthrough.

**My interpretation:** SLAB fits a strategy of creating additional places where energy and industrial production can be colocated under a favorable operating framework.

The potential commercial product would therefore be more than surface area. It would be a package of:

- Usable industrial space.
- Reliable electricity and water.
- Marine access and logistics.
- Shared maintenance and emergency services.
- A clear legal framework.
- A predictable path from signing a contract to beginning production.

A platform could be inexpensive to build and still fail commercially if its tenants cannot obtain power, move feedstocks, insure operations, or repair equipment. Conversely, an expensive platform could be competitive if it removes an especially costly bottleneck.

## 3. Can we actually build something like this?

**Large industrial facilities and substantial concrete structures can operate on water.** The engineering category has real precedents.

| Precedent | Documented achievement | What it establishes |
|---|---|---|
| **Shell Prelude FLNG** | A floating gas-processing facility measuring **488 × 74 meters**, designed to remain moored offshore for at least 25 years. | Complex industrial processes, utilities, and storage can operate offshore. |
| **Japan’s Mega-Float** | A roughly **1,000-meter-long** trial floating airport in Tokyo Bay, with actual takeoffs and landings. | Very large floating surfaces can support demanding operations. |
| **Monaco’s floating dock** | A prestressed-concrete structure displacing approximately **165,000 tons**, with a **100-year design specification**. | Large concrete marine structures can float and be designed for extended service. |

These are documented by the operator, government research program, and builder, respectively. Each solves a more specific problem than SLAB’s complete vision. [About Us](https://www.shell.com.au/about-us/what-we-do/gas-and-lng/prelude-flng-facility/prelude-overview.html)

DNV also publishes a standard specifically addressing the design, construction, and in-service inspection of offshore concrete structures, including floating structures. There is an established engineering framework against which a concrete-based proposal could be evaluated. No evidence located here establishes that SLAB has obtained such a review or uses that particular construction system. [dnv.com](https://www.dnv.com/energy/standards-guidelines/dnv-st-c502-offshore-concrete-structures/)

### How a concrete structure floats

A solid block of ordinary concrete sinks. A hollow concrete hull can float because the average density of the structure, including its enclosed air spaces, is lower than water.

As a purely illustrative calculation, consider a rectangular hull with:

- A waterplane area of 100 × 100 meters.
- A draft of 5 meters.
- Seawater density of approximately 1,025 kilograms per cubic meter.

Its displacement would be about **51,250 metric tonnes**. That must support the hull, equipment, buildings, cargo, liquid inventories, ballast, and occupants. It is not freely available payload.

These are explanatory dimensions, **not SLAB specifications**.

### The difficult engineering problems

**1. Wave-induced motion and bending**

A very large platform can flex as waves pass underneath it. Engineers call the interaction between structural flexibility and water motion *hydroelasticity*. Mega-Float’s research explicitly addressed this problem and its effects on aircraft operations.

A surface that works in a protected bay cannot automatically be transferred to an exposed ocean site. Greater size can reduce some motions while introducing significant structural bending. [mlit.go.jp](https://www.mlit.go.jp/english/maritime/mega_float.html)

**2. Industrial loads and stability**

A refinery tank, a tall building, and a warehouse impose different loads. Filling and emptying tanks changes weight distribution. Moving cargo changes trim. A credible platform needs defined loading limits and an operating plan that keeps it stable as tenants’ activities change.

**3. Moorings and utility connections**

The structure must hold position through wind, waves, and currents while tolerating controlled movement. Its cables, pipelines, boarding systems, and other connections have to accommodate that movement too.

The useful question is whether the hull, moorings, utilities, and equipment remain operational together—not merely whether the structure stays afloat.

**4. Flooding, fire, and industrial accidents**

Compartmentalization and damage stability are essential. A collision or local leak should not threaten the entire platform. Combining hazardous processing with offices, housing, or expensive computing equipment makes separation and emergency planning more demanding.

**5. Construction, inspection, and repair**

A real design needs a fabrication sequence, assembly method, inspection access, and repair strategy. If a platform is too large to use ordinary dry-dock infrastructure, maintaining its underside and critical components becomes a central design problem.

**6. Shore support**

Marine operations still need people, replacement parts, fuel or other supplies, and waste removal. Shell’s description of Prelude includes helicopters and an onshore supply base. A large deck does not remove dependence on terrestrial logistics. [About Us](https://www.shell.com.au/about-us/what-we-do/gas-and-lng/prelude-flng-facility/prelude-overview.html)

### A technical attribution mistake worth avoiding

Some search results make it appear that Luckey disclosed a design using inflatable airforms, ultra-high-performance concrete, geopolymers, and ocean thermal energy conversion.

The full thread places that explanation under **Ben Silone’s reply**, not Luckey’s. I found no confirmation that Silone is part of SLAB. ## 4. How credible is the 1,000-year lifespan?

**It is a design ambition whose meaning and supporting evidence have not been disclosed.**

The first question is what must last 1,000 years:

- The primary structural shell?
- The same material without replacement?
- A maintained platform with replaceable modules?
- An entire functioning industrial facility?

Those are substantially different claims.

A durable hull could coexist with frequent replacement of pumps, seals, cables, cooling equipment, moorings, buildings, and tenant machinery. The maintenance assumptions are therefore part of the lifespan claim.

There is scientific evidence of unusually durable marine concrete. Berkeley Lab’s research on Roman harbor materials describes mineral development that helps explain their longevity. But those structures are not modern, watertight floating hulls exposed to cyclic bending and changing industrial loads. Applying that evidence to SLAB requires its own design and validation. [Berkeley Lab News Center](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2017/07/03/ancient-concrete-could-teach-us-to-do-as-romans-did/)

Modern offshore concrete also has known deterioration mechanisms. Research prepared for Norway’s petroleum safety authority addresses cracking, reinforcement and prestressing corrosion, and other aging issues. [Ageing of Offshore Concrete Structures.doc](https://www.havtil.no/contentassets/24974571fd8442bea4c21d3679d93a8e/osl-804-r04-2---ageing-of-offshore-concrete-structures.pdf)

To evaluate SLAB’s claim, I would want:

1. A material and reinforcement specification.
2. A site-specific environmental exposure model.
3. Crack-control and watertightness criteria.
4. Inspection and repair access.
5. A list of replaceable components.
6. A reliability analysis for extreme conditions.
7. Independent engineering review.

The commercial significance also deserves perspective. In an illustrative model with constant annual benefits and a 5% discount rate, extending those benefits from 100 years to 1,000 years increases present value by only about **0.77%**.

That does not make longevity worthless. It means that reducing maintenance, avoiding replacements, and operating reliably during the first several decades will usually matter more to conventional financing than benefits centuries into the future.

## 5. Does going offshore actually escape state and local regulation?

**It can change the applicable legal regime. It does not automatically eliminate regulation.**

Luckey’s short “three miles” reply is plausibly referring to California’s ordinary offshore boundary. That is different from the boundary of U.S. territorial waters.

### The relevant maritime zones

| Position from the applicable legal baseline | General significance |
|---|---|
| **Within roughly 3 nautical miles off California** | Generally within the state’s offshore submerged-land boundary, alongside federal jurisdiction. |
| **Beyond 3 but within 12 nautical miles** | May be outside California’s ordinary offshore boundary while remaining inside the U.S. territorial sea. |
| **12–24 nautical miles** | The U.S. contiguous zone, with specified customs, fiscal, immigration, and sanitary enforcement powers. |
| **Beyond the territorial sea to 200 nautical miles** | The U.S. exclusive economic zone, with resource rights and jurisdiction over specified offshore installations and environmental matters. |
| **Beyond the EEZ** | High-seas rules may apply to the water column; applicable flag-state and other legal obligations remain. |

These distances use legally defined baselines. Three nautical miles is approximately 3.45 ordinary miles. Texas and Florida’s Gulf coast have different submerged-land boundaries, extending about nine nautical miles. Specific statutes can also retain their own geographic definitions. [National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration](https://www.noaa.gov/maritime-zones-and-boundaries)

A platform four nautical miles off California could therefore be outside the state’s ordinary offshore boundary and still well inside U.S. territorial waters.

### Classification is decisive

Is SLAB a vessel, a stationary installation, an artificial island, or something treated differently under different statutes?

Flotation and towability alone do not settle the answer. In *Lozman v. City of Riviera Beach*, the Supreme Court examined practical design for transportation over water and rejected a definition broad enough to cover essentially everything that floats. [LII / Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/11-626)

For SLAB, important facts would include:

- Whether it is permanently moored.
- How it attaches to the seabed.
- Whether it is practically designed for voyages or transportation.
- What its tenants produce.
- Whether its activities concern offshore resources or energy.

The concept image cannot answer those questions.

### Some state-derived law can apply offshore

The **Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act**, or OCSLA, extends federal law to specified offshore locations and installations. In covered settings, it also adopts qualifying adjacent-state law as federal law. That complicates any blanket claim that state-origin rules disappear. [LII / Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/43/1333)

However, it would also be wrong to say all California law follows a platform offshore.

In *Parker Drilling v. Newton*, the Supreme Court held that state law is not adopted under this mechanism when federal law already addresses the relevant issue. The particular California wage requirements at issue were not imported where federal wage law governed. This is a concrete example of a meaningful regulatory difference, with continuing federal protections. [LII / Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/18-389)

### State influence can persist through permits and connections

Three examples matter:

**Coastal effects.** NOAA explains that federal consistency under the Coastal Zone Management Act can apply to federal actions outside a state’s coastal zone when they have reasonably foreseeable effects on its coastal uses or resources. Relevant federal permits may therefore still involve state coastal policies. [coast.noaa.gov](https://coast.noaa.gov/czm/consistency/)

**Air emissions.** Where EPA’s offshore air regime applies, qualifying sources within 25 nautical miles of a state’s **seaward boundary** can face requirements associated with the corresponding onshore area. That distance is measured from the offshore state boundary, not the beach. Whether the rule covers SLAB depends on its classification and activities. [US EPA](https://www.epa.gov/caa-permitting/outer-continental-shelf-air-permits)

**Water discharges.** The Clean Water Act retains a three-mile statutory definition of territorial seas and distinguishes vessels or floating craft from other sources for certain offshore discharges. It would be inaccurate to assume that every conceivable SLAB design follows the same permitting route—or that offshore discharges are generally unregulated. [US EPA](https://www.epa.gov/cwa-404/clean-water-act-section-502-general-definitions)

Power cables, fiber landings, pipelines, ports, and shore support also create separate onshore permitting relationships.

### The medical ambition is more explicit in his follow-up replies

Luckey subsequently described wanting people to access treatments he considers safe but legally restricted. He cited personalized cancer research moving abroad and emphasized state and local medical rules.

These are his characterizations. The replies do not identify a specific treatment protocol, clinical operator, targeted statute, or accepted exemption, and they do not establish the safety of any treatment. [X post](https://x.com/PalmerLuckey/status/2122907166059143212)

Even genuinely foreign clinical studies used to support U.S. drug approvals face FDA acceptance requirements involving good clinical practice, ethics review, informed consent, and data integrity. Providing treatment offshore, conducting research, importing a drug, and obtaining U.S. marketing approval are separate legal questions. [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/fda-acceptance-foreign-clinical-studies-not-conducted-under-ind-frequently-asked-questions)

### Supply chains and sovereignty

CBP explains that Jones Act coastwise rules can extend to qualifying OCS points. Depending on SLAB’s status, merchandise transportation between the platform and a U.S. port could face those restrictions. [cbp.gov](https://www.cbp.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/Jones%20Act%20ICP_Complete_04DEC24.pdf)

Artificial islands also do not automatically create sovereign territory. UNCLOS Article 60 says they do not acquire natural-island status or territorial seas of their own. The United States is not a treaty party, but NOAA describes its observance of relevant Law of the Sea rules as reflected in customary international law and practice. [un.org](https://www.un.org/depts/los/convention_agreements/texts/unclos/unclos_e.pdf?external_link=true)

**The credible legal opportunity is a clearly defined arrangement that removes particular constraints.** Its value would need to be established industry by industry.

## 6. Could SLAB be cheaper than land?

There is not enough disclosed information to calculate that.

The comparison must include equivalent services and operating performance. For an offshore facility, that means:

> Hull and marine infrastructure + utilities + logistics + maintenance + insurance + financing + tenant adaptations.

For a land-based facility, it means:

> Property + site preparation + utilities + approvals and schedule costs + ongoing operations.

Comparing the price of a floating surface with the price of a parcel would leave out much of the customer’s actual cost.

### Where the economics could work

My assessment is that the strongest case would have several favorable conditions together:

- Scarce or difficult-to-develop industrial sites near the desired market.
- Accessible electricity and marine logistics.
- A substantial advantage in approval time or operating rules.
- A tenant whose products and feedstocks suit the location.
- A design that can be maintained and insured economically.

The weaker case would be a process that can use inexpensive inland land near power, rail, and a workforce.

A cheap hull could lose once offshore operating expenses are included. A costly hull could win if it removes a major delay or capacity constraint. Neither outcome has been demonstrated for SLAB.

The first meaningful commercial result should report **all-in customer performance**: time to operations, availability, delivered energy price, maintenance effort, and cost per unit of production.

## 7. The AI data center opportunity

AI computing is a plausible tenant because its output can leave electronically and its cooling system can use a large water body. It also exposes one of the project’s biggest unanswered questions: electricity.

### Floating computing already has a precedent

Nautilus’s current Stockton specifications list **6.5 MW of critical IT load** on a floating facility. Black & Veatch describes commissioning the project as a 7 MW facility. Those are different descriptions, so the figures should not be treated as interchangeable measures of usable IT capacity. The installation demonstrates commercial computing on a moored barge with supporting infrastructure. [Nautilus Data Technologies](https://nautilusdt.com/stockton-data-center/)

Microsoft’s Natick experiment placed **864 servers** in a sealed subsea unit and reported lower failure rates than a land comparison. Microsoft investigated the controlled atmosphere and lack of human disturbance as possible explanations. That result concerns a particular sealed installation; it cannot be transferred automatically to occupied server halls on a floating platform. [Microsoft Research: Facts And Figures](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/project/natick/facts-and-figures/)

### Cooling is useful, but still engineered

The ocean can be a heat sink. A practical system still needs pumps, heat exchangers, filtration, corrosion control, intake and discharge design, backup capacity, and maintenance.

Seawater also needs to be distinguished from the clean coolant circulating through IT equipment. Nautilus’s documented system separates its external water loop from its internal cooling loop through heat exchangers. [Nautilus Data Technologies](https://nautilusdt.com/stockton-data-center/)

Waste heat is transferred to the environment rather than eliminated. Reduced evaporative freshwater consumption does not establish zero ecological impact.

### Power is the decisive missing piece

SLAB has not publicly identified a generating system, utility interconnection, or electricity contract.

A cable from shore can preserve dependence on land-based grid capacity. Fuel generation introduces fuel logistics and emissions. Renewable generation requires a plan for variability. Nuclear, wave, or ocean-thermal systems would each add substantial engineering and regulatory work; none should be attributed to SLAB without evidence.

Consider this **illustrative scenario**, rather than a SLAB forecast:

- IT load: **100 MW**.
- Power usage effectiveness: **1.20**.
- Continuous operation throughout the year.

PUE is total facility energy divided by IT energy. [energy.gov](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2024-07/best-practice-guide-data-center-design.pdf)

| Measure | Illustrative result |
|---|---:|
| Total facility demand | **120 MW** |
| Annual electricity | **1.0512 TWh** |
| Annual energy cost at 6¢/kWh | **$63.1 million** |
| Annual energy cost at 10¢/kWh | **$105.1 million** |
| Annual energy cost at 15¢/kWh | **$157.7 million** |
| Effect of each 1¢/kWh difference | **About $10.5 million annually** |

These are calculated scenarios, not current electricity quotations. They show why delivered energy price can outweigh land savings.

The thermal scale is substantial too. Rejecting approximately 120 MW of heat to seawater, assuming a 10°C temperature rise and typical volumetric heat capacity, would require around **2.9 cubic meters per second** of once-through water flow. A 5°C rise would require roughly twice that flow.

That is a heat-balance illustration, not a proposed permitted discharge temperature. The water is circulated and returned rather than necessarily consumed.

### Networking and maintenance

Offshore location does not inherently prevent large-model training if the tightly connected compute cluster is colocated on the platform. NVIDIA’s reference architecture distinguishes internal compute, storage, and management networks; those local functions remain essential regardless of where the facility sits. [docs.nvidia.com](https://docs.nvidia.com/dgx-superpod/reference-architecture/scalable-infrastructure-h200/latest/network-fabrics.html)

Distributing tightly coupled training across platforms with constrained links is a different proposition. Selected inference and batch workloads may tolerate more flexibility, but satellite connectivity is not automatically equivalent to redundant fiber.

For a compute customer, I would prioritize evidence about:

- Delivered electricity price and reliability.
- Commissioned IT capacity.
- Fiber-route diversity.
- Hardware protection and cooling performance.
- Repair access and spare parts.
- Weather-related service interruptions.
- Actual availability and useful compute utilization.

Cheap energy has limited value if expensive hardware spends too much time unavailable.

## 8. How SLAB compares with Panthalassa and other ocean-computing concepts

The projects often grouped together as “ocean data centers” solve different problems:

| Approach | Primary engineering objective |
|---|---|
| **Nautilus-style moored barge** | Operate a data center on water while using surrounding water for cooling and retaining shore connections. |
| **Microsoft Natick-style subsea module** | Operate computing equipment in a sealed underwater environment with limited human intervention. |
| **Panthalassa-style autonomous node** | Generate power from waves and consume it onboard for computing. |
| **SLAB’s stated ambition** | Provide broadly useful offshore industrial capacity for multiple sectors. |

Panthalassa announced a **$140 million Series B in May 2026** for its wave-powered computing program. It described an Ocean-3 pilot and targeted commercial deployments in 2027 at that time. Those were company development plans, rather than proof of achieved commercial performance. [prnewswire.com](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/panthalassa-raises-140-million-to-power-ai-at-sea-302761078.html)

SLAB’s potentially distinctive contribution would be a general-purpose industrial platform with unusually durable infrastructure and a repeatable deployment model.

Existing projects reduce uncertainty about individual building blocks. They do not establish that SLAB has solved the combination.

## 9. Which industries look most plausible initially?

This is my assessment of operating characteristics, not a disclosed rollout plan.

| Use | Potential advantage | Main obstacle |
|---|---|---|
| **Data centers** | Output leaves electronically; water offers cooling potential. | Reliable power, connectivity, uptime, and maintenance. |
| **Desalination** | Immediate access to seawater. | Intake and brine effects, energy, and water delivery to buyers. |
| **Marine logistics** | Direct access to vessels and offshore activity. | Site-specific demand, navigation, and port arrangements. |
| **Chemical processing or refining** | Marine feedstock supply and shared utilities could be useful. | Process safety, emissions, storage, and hazardous cargo handling. |
| **Metal processing** | Potential value where power and siting dominate. | Heavy material flows, concentrated loads, electricity, and waste. |
| **Medical services and research** | Possible differences in operating jurisdiction. | Legal authority, clinical standards, emergency transfer, and acceptance of results. |

**A specialized first installation with an anchor customer appears more credible than launching the entire mixed-use industrial city at once.**

That approach would allow the company to demonstrate a manageable set of advantages, measure operating costs, and expand from evidence.

## 10. Environmental and national-industrial implications

An offshore platform could reduce demand for scarce coastal land or evaporative cooling water. It could also create different impacts through construction materials, moorings, intakes, heat or brine discharge, shipping, fuel use, and accidental releases.

The actual balance depends on the site, tenants, and design. I found no SLAB environmental assessment.

These questions also affect commercial performance. Intake restrictions can limit cooling capacity. Weather can delay repairs. A different discharge arrangement can change pump power and capital cost. A meaningful comparison needs equivalent output and reliability onshore and offshore.

Luckey’s national-industrial argument is explicit in the announcement: he wants SLAB to strengthen American production. The potential benefit would be additional places to build near U.S. markets and infrastructure when land-based options are constrained.

That benefit would have to appear in actual output, reliability, costs, and integration with suppliers and workers. A platform does not automatically solve feedstock dependence, workforce shortages, or equipment supply constraints. Concentrating important activities offshore can also create common risks in power, communications, and marine access.

The strongest version of the idea lowers the total cost of useful industrial activity. Its value would be less convincing if it depended mainly on moving costs or risks onto others.

## 11. What evidence would make SLAB substantially more credible?

The public record is currently strongest on intention and weakest on execution. The most useful next disclosures would be:

1. **A first site and purpose.** A location, water depth, environmental design basis, and initial use.
2. **A clear project organization.** Entity, accountable management, engineering partners, and committed capital.
3. **A reviewable design.** Hull form, dimensions, materials, payload limits, mooring, damage stability, and utility interfaces.
4. **Independent assessment.** Classification engagement or equivalent engineering review, with its scope clearly stated.
5. **Evidence behind longevity.** Materials testing, deterioration models, inspection access, and replacement strategy.
6. **A documented regulatory route.** Agency positions addressing the actual configuration and each intended activity.
7. **A costed construction and operating plan.** Including marine support, maintenance, insurance, financing, and schedule dependencies.
8. **An anchor customer and utilities.** Credible demand matched with power, data, water, feedstock, and transport arrangements.
9. **An instrumented pilot.** Measured motion, loads, water ingress, maintenance requirements, power, cooling, and uptime.
10. **A commercial result.** A customer operating competitively under the expected legal regime.

For computing, the key outcome is dependable useful computation at a competitive total cost. For processing, it is dependable production at a competitive cost per unit. Those measures would say much more than another rendering or valuation announcement.

## 12. My overall assessment

**The engineering category is established.** Large offshore industrial plants, floating concrete structures, and water-based computing installations exist.

**SLAB’s specific implementation remains largely undisclosed.** The available evidence does not support a responsible estimate of construction cost, opening date, operating capacity, or probability of commercial success.

**The legal strategy could produce meaningful advantages.** Offshore location can alter which rules apply. Whether those differences are valuable depends on the structure, activity, site, and agency interpretation.

**The 1,000-year target requires a much more precise explanation.** A durable primary structure with replaceable systems is understandable as an engineering ambition. Its validation would require material, maintenance, and reliability evidence.

**The commercial opportunity is conditional but substantial in principle.** A platform that gives industrial customers reliable utilities, predictable approvals, and competitive operating costs could be valuable. Its strongest initial form would likely be focused and specialized.

I would follow SLAB as an early-disclosed industrial infrastructure venture. **The decisive milestone would be a real, independently reviewed, legally defined installation on which a first customer can operate successfully.** That would turn the broad vision into evidence that additional American industrial capacity can be built this way.
